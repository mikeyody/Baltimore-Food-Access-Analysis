## Summary

This analysis combines 11 public datasets (USDA food access and SNAP retailer data, CDC PLACES health estimates, Census ACS 2020-2024 and TIGER/Line boundaries, and Baltimore City open data) for all 199 census tracts in Baltimore City (573,243 residents). It measures how far each neighborhood is from a full-service grocery store (Supermarket, Super Store), how that relates to income, car ownership and diet-related disease, and where new food resources would reach the most people in need.

## Key findings

**1. About 43% of Baltimore residents live in a low-income tract more than half a mile from a full-service grocery store.** That is 244,920 people in 84 of 199 tracts, using 2026 store locations. At the 1-mile standard USDA uses for urban areas, 12 tracts (31,150 residents) qualify. The city has 49 full-service grocery stores; the median tract's population center is 0.54 miles from the nearest one.

**2. Car-free households bear the access gap.** 37,681 households without a vehicle live in tracts more than half a mile from a full-service grocery (4,499 in tracts more than a mile away). 26 tracts are both more than half a mile away and have at least 40% of households without a car, home to 14,900 car-free households.

**3. Low-income, low-access tracts have markedly higher rates of diet-related disease.** Compared with other tracts, adults there have higher rates of diabetes (18.1% vs 13.2%), obesity (43.1% vs 36.8%), high blood pressure (45.9% vs 37.3%) and coronary heart disease (7.7% vs 5.8%) - every gap statistically significant.

**4. Income, not distance, explains most of that gap.** Accounting for median household income shrinks the link between distance and health by 44-68%; income alone explains 48%-61% of the variation in disease rates between tracts. After income, distance remains a statistically significant correlate of high blood pressure and coronary heart disease (for high blood pressure, +5.2 points per mile, 95% CI +0.8 to +9.5). Once the share of residents aged 65+ is also accounted for, none of these associations remains significant at p < 0.05. This is correlation, not causation.

**5. A short, stable priority list.** The ten highest-need tracts, led by Dunbar-Broadway / Oldtown, South Clifton Park / Darley Park and Oldtown, are home to 22,288 residents and 5,753 car-free households. 6 of them stay in the top 10 under all 5 weighting scenarios tested. The map has also shifted since USDA's 2019 data: 23 tracts (51,468 residents) are low-income and low-access now but were not flagged then, while 30 flagged in 2019 no longer meet the 2026 test.

## Recommendations

1. **Focus new full-service grocery investment and pilots on the most robust high-need neighborhoods: Dunbar-Broadway / Oldtown, South Clifton Park / Darley Park, Oldtown, Harlem Park / Sandtown-Winchester, Cherry Hill and Arlington / Woodmere.** These rank in the top 10 under every weighting tested, so the case for them doesn't depend on how priorities are weighed. Where a new store isn't viable, mobile markets, produce stands or a grocery partnership at an existing community anchor can fill the gap.

2. **Close the transportation gap for car-free households.** Target the 26 tracts where at least 40% of households have no car and the nearest full-service grocery is more than half a mile away (14,900 car-free households) with a grocery shuttle, transit stops or service changes that connect to the nearest full-service stores, and SNAP-online grocery delivery outreach.

3. **Pair access with affordability and health programs.** Because income explains most of the health gap, a store alone is unlikely to move disease rates. Combine access projects with SNAP enrollment, healthy-food incentives that stretch SNAP dollars on produce, healthy corner-store programs (95% of low-income, low-access tracts have a SNAP-authorized store of some kind within half a mile), and clinic-based produce prescriptions in the tracts with the highest diabetes and blood-pressure rates.

## Limitations

- **Correlation, not causation.** The data are cross-sectional and at tract level; they can't show that distance causes disease, and tract averages don't describe individuals.
- **Modeled health data.** CDC PLACES estimates are model-based and partly predicted from tract demographics, which builds in some correlation with income.
- **Straight-line distance from one point per tract.** Real walking routes are longer, and the population-weighted center hides variation within a tract.
- **Store classification.** Full-service stores come from USDA's SNAP store types, with warehouse clubs and gas stations removed; stores that don't accept SNAP are missed. Store quality, prices and hours aren't measured.
- **Mixed vintages.** USDA's supermarket-based 2019 flags were converted from 2010 to 2020 tracts by land area, and ACS figures are 2020-2024 averages with margins of error.
- **Relative need score.** The score ranks tracts against each other within Baltimore; 2 tracts with too little data were not scored.

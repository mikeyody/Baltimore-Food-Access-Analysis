## Q4. Does food access relate to health once income is accounted for?

Method: ordinary least squares regression across 195 tracts with complete data, HC3 robust standard errors. Outcome = tract health rate (%); predictors = distance (miles) from the population-weighted center to the nearest full-service grocery store, and the log of median household income. Partial correlations remove the linear effect of income from both distance and the health rate, then correlate what is left. (Partial-correlation p-values use classical standard errors and regression p-values use robust ones, so the two can differ slightly near the 0.05 line.) Census caps median income at $250,000+, which affects only the richest few tracts.

### In plain English

**Overall:** distance to a full-service grocery store is associated with worse health on its own, but accounting for income shrinks those associations by 44-68%. Income is by far the stronger predictor. After income, the distance association remains statistically significant (p < 0.05) for high blood pressure and coronary heart disease; after also accounting for age, it is significant for none of the outcomes. Food access is therefore a modest, secondary correlate of health in Baltimore, layered on top of a much larger income gradient.

- **Diabetes.** On its own, each extra mile between a tract's population center and the nearest full-service grocery store goes with a +4.85-point difference in the diabetes rate (p = 0.002). After accounting for income, the association shrinks to +2.31 points per mile (95% CI -0.00 to +4.62, p = 0.050): borderline evidence (just above or near the usual p < 0.05 cut-off), so the association is suggestive rather than clear. Income alone explains 57% of the variation between tracts; adding distance brings that to 58%. A tract with 10% higher median income has a -0.88-point difference in the diabetes rate at the same distance. Also controlling for the share of residents 65+, the distance association is +1.85 points per mile (p = 0.099, borderline). Partial correlation of distance with diabetes, controlling for income: r = +0.15 (p = 0.036).
- **Obesity.** On its own, each extra mile between a tract's population center and the nearest full-service grocery store goes with a +5.05-point difference in the obesity rate (p = 0.011). After accounting for income, the association shrinks to +1.60 points per mile (95% CI -1.31 to +4.51, p = 0.282): not statistically distinguishable from zero: once income is known, distance adds little. Income alone explains 61% of the variation between tracts; adding distance brings that to 61%. A tract with 10% higher median income has a -1.20-point difference in the obesity rate at the same distance. Also controlling for the share of residents 65+, the distance association is +1.66 points per mile (p = 0.266, not significant). Partial correlation of distance with obesity, controlling for income: r = +0.08 (p = 0.242).
- **High blood pressure.** On its own, each extra mile between a tract's population center and the nearest full-service grocery store goes with a +9.28-point difference in the high blood pressure rate (p < 0.001). After accounting for income, the association shrinks to +5.18 points per mile (95% CI +0.85 to +9.52, p = 0.019): a statistically significant association: tracts farther from a store have higher rates than tracts with similar incomes. Income alone explains 48% of the variation between tracts; adding distance brings that to 50%. A tract with 10% higher median income has a -1.43-point difference in the high blood pressure rate at the same distance. Also controlling for the share of residents 65+, the distance association is +3.90 points per mile (p = 0.054, borderline). Partial correlation of distance with high blood pressure, controlling for income: r = +0.17 (p = 0.015).
- **Coronary heart disease.** On its own, each extra mile between a tract's population center and the nearest full-service grocery store goes with a +2.07-point difference in the coronary heart disease rate (p < 0.001). After accounting for income, the association shrinks to +1.15 points per mile (95% CI +0.20 to +2.09, p = 0.017): a statistically significant association: tracts farther from a store have higher rates than tracts with similar incomes. Income alone explains 50% of the variation between tracts; adding distance brings that to 51%. A tract with 10% higher median income has a -0.32-point difference in the coronary heart disease rate at the same distance. Also controlling for the share of residents 65+, the distance association is +0.84 points per mile (p = 0.056, borderline). Partial correlation of distance with coronary heart disease, controlling for income: r = +0.18 (p = 0.013).

**This is correlation, not causation.** Tracts far from supermarkets differ in many ways (age, race, housing, transit, smoking, healthcare access) that this model doesn't capture, and CDC PLACES health estimates are themselves partly modeled from tract demographics, which builds in a link to income. A significant association would mean distance and health move together after accounting for income, not that opening a store would change health rates.
